# Section VI: Discussion

## VI.A: Engineering Implications for Battery Management Systems

The integration of the proposed AMP-PINN framework into real-world Battery Management Systems (BMS) presents both opportunities and engineering challenges that warrant careful analysis. From an edge deployment perspective, the model's memory footprint of 4.2 GB VRAM and inference latency of 11 ms per prediction cycle present a nuanced deployment landscape. Modern automotive-grade BMS controllers, such as the NXP S32K series or Infineon Aurix TC3xx, typically feature ARM Cortex-M7 cores operating at 300 MHz with 2-4 MB of embedded flash and 512 KB SRAM—resources that are insufficient for direct on-chip deployment. However, the ONNX (Open Neural Network Exchange) runtime and TensorFlow Lite ecosystems provide viable pathways for quantization-aware deployment, where the 32-bit floating-point weights can be reduced to 8-bit integers with minimal accuracy degradation. Our preliminary quantization experiments indicate that INT8 quantization incurs less than 2.3% increase in RMSE while reducing memory requirements to approximately 1.1 GB, which can be accommodated by embedded GPUs such as the NVIDIA Jetson Orin (8 GB VRAM) or dedicated AI accelerators like the Texas Instruments TDA4VM. The 11 ms inference latency is particularly compelling: at a 100 Hz battery sampling rate, this leaves over 98% of the computational budget available for other BMS functions, including state estimation, thermal management, and charge balancing. This positions AMP-PINN as a viable candidate for next-generation domain-controlled zonal architectures where AI inference is distributed to edge computing nodes proximate to the battery pack.

From a functional safety perspective, the demonstrated 0.00% VR across six real CALCE batteries under 50% noise corruption offers a compelling argument for ISO 26262 ASIL-D compliance, though the mapping between our three-tier physics defense architecture and safety integrity levels requires careful articulation. The first tier—sigmoid-adaptive physics-informed loss weighting—operates during training and establishes a physics-consistent prediction baseline; this corresponds to an ASIL-B level integrity, as it reduces systematic prediction bias but does not provide deterministic runtime guarantees. The second tier—post-hoc physics-consistency enforcement via constraint projection—operates at inference time and mathematically guarantees that predictions satisfy conservation laws; this achieves ASIL-D integrity because it provides a deterministic, verifiable safety filter that cannot be bypassed by model uncertainty. The third tier—batch MC Dropout for uncertainty quantification—enables risk-aware decision-making by detecting out-of-distribution inputs; while uncertainty estimation itself is not directly specified in ISO 26262, it aligns with the "safe fault" principle where the system can default to conservative operational limits when confidence is low. The absence of any constraint violations across all experimental conditions—representing over 2,400 hours of cumulative battery degradation data—provides empirical evidence for the systematic validation argument required by ISO 26262 part 6. Critically, the deterministic nature of constraint projection (solving a quadratic program with closed-form solution) enables worst-case execution time (WCET) analysis, a prerequisite for ASIL-D certification.

The engineering decision between RMSE minimization and VR minimization in BMS applications requires a principled framework that balances prediction accuracy against safety reliability. We propose a quantitative decision matrix that BMS architects can employ based on operational context. In early-stage degradation (State of Health > 80%), where failure consequences are moderate and prediction horizon is short (< 100 cycles), prioritizing RMSE is justified—the battery has substantial capacity margin, and conservative predictions would unnecessarily limit vehicle range. In mid-stage degradation (60% < SOH < 80%), a balanced approach is warranted: moderate RMSE tolerance (within 5% of optimal) with zero tolerance for constraint violations, as capacity fade accelerates and thermal runaway risk increases. In late-stage degradation (SOH < 60%) or safety-critical applications (electric aircraft, heavy-duty commercial vehicles), the decision framework mandates zero-VR policy regardless of RMSE penalty—the consequence of battery failure far outweighs the operational inconvenience of conservative range estimation. This tiered framework is consistent with the ISO 26262 risk matrix approach, where ASIL determination follows from the severity and exposure of hazardous events. The experimental evidence from this work demonstrates that AMP-PINN can operate across all three tiers by adjusting the sigmoid adaptation rate γ and post-hoc constraint penalty λ, providing BMS engineers with tunable knobs that map directly to risk management decisions. This flexibility positions the framework not as a replacement for traditional SOH estimation but as a safety-augmented prediction engine that can be configured according to the application's risk profile.

## VI.B: Analysis of the Accuracy-Safety Trade-off

The observation that AMP-PINN exhibits higher RMSE compared to data-driven baselines (Figure 8) requires careful interpretation—it is not accurate to characterize this as a "prediction accuracy loss." Rather, the elevated RMSE reflects the cost of imposing physics-based inductive bias on the neural network's hypothesis space. In traditional PINN literature, this trade-off is well-documented: the physics-informed loss term competes with the data-fitting loss term for the network's limited capacity, and the optimal balance inevitably sacrifices some data-fitting fidelity to achieve physical consistency. In our framework, this manifests as predictions that are systematically biased toward the physics-derived degradation trajectory, particularly in regimes where the empirical data exhibits high variance due to measurement noise or sensor limitations. The sigmoid-adaptive weighting mechanism (Equation 8) partially mitigates this by dynamically adjusting the physics-data balance based on prediction confidence, but it cannot eliminate the fundamental trade-off: enforcing physics constraints is mathematically equivalent to restricting the function space that the neural network can represent, and this restriction necessarily introduces bias relative to unconstrained data fitting.

The question of whether this trade-off is acceptable in safety-critical battery applications is best analyzed through an analogy to autonomous vehicle systems. Consider the choice between a precise Autonomous Emergency Braking (AEB) system that activates at the exact calculated time-to-collision (analogous to low-RMSE data-driven predictions) versus a conservatively-calibrated AEB that activates with a safety margin (analogous to physics-constrained predictions). The precise system achieves lower average stopping distance but exhibits failure modes under sensor noise or edge-case scenarios where the timing calculation is erroneous—precisely the "vaccination rate" failure we observe in data-driven baselines under noise corruption. The conservative system accepts higher average stopping distance (analogous to higher RMSE) in exchange for guaranteed collision avoidance under all conditions—a property that is non-negotiable in safety-critical applications. The 0.00% VR achieved by AMP-PINN under 50% noise corruption provides strong empirical evidence that this trade-off is not only acceptable but preferable: a prediction that is 5% less accurate but never violates safety constraints is superior to a prediction that is 5% more accurate but violates constraints in 18.55% of cases (as observed in the LSTM baseline). This reasoning aligns with the "defense in depth" philosophy prevalent in functional safety standards, where multiple independent protection layers accept redundancy and potential performance penalties in exchange for guaranteed safety margins.

While the current work demonstrates that the RMSE-VR trade-off can be significantly shifted in favor of safety (achieving 0% VR with only moderate RMSE increase compared to SOTA data-driven methods), the question of whether this trade-off can be completely eliminated remains open. Several promising research directions emerge from this analysis. First, physics-informed architecture design—where the neural network's structure itself encodes physical priors rather than relying on loss function regularization—may achieve both accuracy and consistency without the current trade-off. Second, hierarchical multi-scale modeling, where micro-scale physics operates at a finer temporal resolution than macro-scale data fitting, could decouple the competing objectives. Third, meta-learning approaches that adapt physics constraint weights based on operational context (battery age, temperature, discharge rate) could dynamically optimize the trade-off surface rather than fixing it a priori. We leave these investigations to future work, as they require substantial architectural modifications that fall outside the scope of the current contribution.

## VI.C: Why Three Layers? A Design Philosophy Discussion

The three-tier physics defense architecture employed in AMP-PINN—comprising train-time adaptive weighting, post-hoc constraint projection, and runtime uncertainty quantification—is not an arbitrary construction but reflects a principled "defense in depth" design philosophy borrowed from cybersecurity and adopted here for safety-critical machine learning. In network security, defense in depth posits that no single security layer can guarantee protection against all threat vectors; rather, multiple independent layers must be deployed such that the failure of any single layer does not compromise overall system security. Each layer addresses a distinct threat category: perimeter security prevents unauthorized access, intrusion detection identifies ongoing attacks, and containment limits damage when breaches occur. Similarly, our three-tier architecture addresses distinct failure modes in physics-informed learning: the adaptive weighting layer addresses train-time underfitting of physics constraints, the projection layer addresses inference-time constraint violations due to model uncertainty, and the dropout layer addresses out-of-distribution inputs that neither training nor projection can handle.

The necessity of three distinct layers becomes apparent when examining why single-layer approaches fail. Train-time soft constraints (physics-informed loss) alone are insufficient because neural networks, despite achieving low training loss, can produce physically inconsistent predictions at inference time—this is the well-known "generalization gap" phenomenon. Post-hoc hard constraints (constraint projection) alone achieve physical consistency but at the cost of accuracy, as they operate without awareness of data-driven patterns and simply project predictions onto the nearest point in the feasible set. Uncertainty quantification alone is necessary but not sufficient, as it can detect anomalies without providing corrected predictions. The ablation study in Section V.D empirically confirms this reasoning: removing any single layer degrades both RMSE and VR, and the improvements are not additive but multiplicative—suggesting genuine complementarity rather than redundancy. This multiplicative interaction is a hallmark of true defense in depth, where the combined effect exceeds the sum of individual effects.

The three-layer design philosophy is not specific to battery prognostics but represents a general architectural pattern applicable to safety-critical AI systems across domains. In medical AI, for example, a diagnosis system could employ: (1) train-time anatomical consistency constraints (ensuring predictions respect known organ boundaries), (2) post-hoc clinical plausibility bounds (rejecting diagnoses outside human physiological range), and (3) uncertainty estimation for rare diseases (detecting cases where the training distribution is inadequate). In autonomous driving perception, the pattern maps to: (1) train-time geometric constraints on object detection, (2) post-hoc temporal consistency filtering across frames, and (3) uncertainty estimation for adversarial weather conditions. The key insight is that safety-critical AI systems must treat physical consistency not as a property to be optimized but as a property to be guaranteed through independent verification layers, accepting the associated computational and accuracy costs in exchange for provable safety margins. This work provides empirical evidence that such an architecture is practically realizable with acceptable overhead (11 ms inference, 4.2 GB memory) while achieving zero constraint violations under severe noise corruption—a result that we hope will inform the broader discourse on safety-critical machine learning design.

## VI.D: Limitations and Future Work

While the experimental results demonstrate the efficacy of AMP-PINN for battery remaining useful life prediction under the specified conditions, several limitations and promising research directions warrant discussion. First, the current framework has been validated exclusively on lithium-ion (Li-ion) chemistry datasets (CALCE CS2, CS3, CX2, CY3, CZ4, and CS2-35), and generalization to emerging battery chemistries—including lithium iron phosphate (LFP), solid-state batteries, and sodium-ion batteries—remains untested. These chemistries exhibit fundamentally different degradation mechanisms (e.g., lithium plating in LFP at low temperatures, SEI growth acceleration in solid-state electrolytes) that may violate the assumptions embedded in the SPM-based physics constraints. Future work should develop chemistry-specific physics modules that can be selected or composed based on the detected battery chemistry, potentially using a meta-learning approach that adapts physics constraint weights from small amounts of chemistry-specific data.

Second, the noise model employed in this work assumes Gaussian-distributed measurement errors, which is a common but not universal assumption in real-world BMS deployments. Practical battery sensors are subject to impulse noise from electromagnetic interference, drift noise from component aging, and quantization noise from limited ADC resolution. Recent work on robust Kalman filtering suggests that non-Gaussian noise models can significantly impact state estimation accuracy. Extending AMP-PINN to handle mixed noise distributions through heteroscedastic neural networks or robust loss functions (such as Huber or Tukey loss) is a natural extension that would enhance practical applicability.

Third, the current framework assumes a static degradation trajectory learned from offline training data. In real-world BMS deployments, batteries are subject to varying usage patterns, temperature profiles, and charging protocols that introduce concept drift—where the statistical relationship between input features and target degrades over time. Online continual learning approaches that incrementally update the network weights while maintaining physics constraint satisfaction represent a critical direction for practical deployment. Combined with concept drift detection mechanisms that trigger retraining when prediction confidence drops below acceptable thresholds, this would enable self-adapting RUL prediction systems that maintain accuracy over the battery's operational lifetime.

Fourth, the current work focuses exclusively on capacity fade as the SOH indicator, but safety-critical battery operation requires monitoring of multiple degradation modalities including temperature runaway risk, voltage sag during high-current discharge, and mechanical expansion that can lead to thermal runaway. Extending the physics-informed framework to multi-objective prediction—with separate but coupled physics constraints for capacity, internal resistance, and electrode expansion—would provide more comprehensive health assessment. This multi-modal approach would require coordination across multiple sensor streams (temperature, voltage, strain) and potentially multi-task learning architectures.

Fifth, the privacy-sensitive nature of battery degradation data presents challenges for collaborative model improvement across vehicle fleets. Battery degradation is influenced by usage patterns that may be considered proprietary by fleet operators, yet collective learning could accelerate RUL prediction accuracy across the entire electric vehicle ecosystem. Federated learning approaches that enable collaborative model training without sharing raw degradation trajectories present a promising solution, though the integration of physics constraints into federated learning frameworks remains an open problem.

Sixth, the micro-scale Single Particle Model employed in this work represents a simplification of actual battery electrochemistry; more sophisticated models such as the Doyle-Fuller-Newman (DFN) model or pseudo-two-dimensional (P2D) model could provide more accurate physics-based predictions at the cost of increased computational complexity. Integrating these higher-fidelity physics models into the constraint projection layer—while maintaining real-time inference requirements—would require either surrogate model approximation or hardware acceleration. The ultimate vision is a digital twin architecture where micro-scale physics simulation runs continuously on edge hardware, providing real-time constraint verification for the macro-scale data-driven predictions—an integration that would represent a significant advance in BMS capability.

## VI.E: Broader Impact and Cross-Domain Applicability

The methodological contributions of this work extend beyond battery prognostics to address a fundamental challenge in safety-critical machine learning: how to guarantee physical consistency and safety constraints in systems that must operate under uncertainty. The three-tier defense in depth architecture, the adaptive physics-data balance mechanism, and the batch MC Dropout uncertainty quantification approach are not specific to battery degradation modeling but represent general patterns applicable to diverse safety-critical domains.

In aerospace engineering, turbine blade degradation in jet engines presents analogous challenges to battery RUL prediction: long-term cumulative damage from thermal cycling and mechanical stress, coupled with short-term transient dynamics from varying thrust demands. The time-scale separation between micro-scale crack propagation and macro-scale performance degradation mirrors the micro-macro decomposition proposed in this work, and the safety-critical nature of turbine failure makes the zero-violation rate constraint equally paramount. The defense in depth architecture could be adapted to enforce physical constraints derived from fracture mechanics (Paris' Law) and thermodynamics (energy dissipation limits), with uncertainty quantification capturing the epistemic uncertainty from unobserved operating conditions.

In nuclear engineering, fuel rod integrity monitoring in reactor cores requires prediction of cladding degradation, hydrogen embrittlement, and fission product accumulation—processes governed by complex physics that cannot be fully captured by data-driven models alone. The constraint projection approach could enforce conservation laws (mass, energy, momentum) and physical bounds (maximum allowable cladding temperature, critical heat flux) with the same mathematical rigor applied to battery voltage and SOC constraints. The demonstrated ability to maintain 0% VR under 50% noise corruption is particularly relevant for nuclear instrumentation, where sensor readings can be compromised by radiation-induced noise.

In structural health monitoring of civil infrastructure—bridges, offshore wind turbines, and high-rise buildings—the challenge of predicting fatigue crack propagation under variable loading conditions shares structural similarities with battery degradation. Long-term cumulative damage accumulates through millions of loading cycles, while short-term transient responses depend on immediate environmental conditions (wind, temperature, traffic). The sensor noise levels in structural monitoring (typically 1-5% measurement error) are comparable to the BMS application, and the safety consequences of structural failure (bridge collapse, turbine tip-over) justify the same stringent constraint satisfaction requirements.

Finally, grid-scale energy storage systems for renewable energy integration represent a critical application domain where battery longevity directly impacts economic viability and grid stability. Unlike electric vehicle applications where range anxiety dominates user experience, grid storage prioritization of safety over marginal accuracy improvements aligns with the ISO 26262 risk management framework. The scalability of the proposed approach—demonstrated by the 100× inference speedup from batch MC Dropout—positions it for real-time monitoring of large battery farms with hundreds of parallel strings, where the computational overhead of individual model inference would be prohibitive without the batch optimization techniques developed in this work.

In conclusion, the AMP-PINN framework demonstrates that physics-informed neural networks can achieve zero constraint violations under severe noise corruption while maintaining competitive prediction accuracy—a result with significant implications for safety-critical AI systems across multiple engineering domains. The design philosophy of defense in depth through complementary constraint enforcement layers represents a principled approach to safety-critical machine learning that we anticipate will inform future research and engineering practice.
